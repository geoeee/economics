Mercantilism / 重商主义

The age of colonial empires, zero-sum trade thinking, and the birth of the joint-stock company — when European states treated national economies as instruments of geopolitical power.

📈 Economics / 经济学

What Was Mercantilism? / 什么是重商主义？

Mercantilism was the dominant economic doctrine from ~1500 to ~1760. Its core belief: national wealth = national power, and national power = accumulation of gold and silver (bullion). Trade was seen as zero-sum — one nation's surplus was another's deficit. The state's role was to maximize exports, minimize imports, accumulate precious metals, and manage the economy like a military campaign. This wasn't just an intellectual position — it was the operating system of European empire-building.

Core Policies / 核心政策

BullionismMaximize gold/silver holdings. Colonies existed to provide bullion. Spain's American silver flooded Europe — and caused the Price Revolution (~150% inflation 1500–1650).
ProtectionismHigh tariffs on manufactured imports; low tariffs on raw material imports. Export manufactured goods, import raw materials. Colonies as captive markets.
Navigation ActsEngland required trade with colonies to use English ships with English crews. Created a state-enforced monopoly on colonial trade.
Chartered MonopoliesState-granted monopoly rights to trading companies (East India Companies). The state outsourced colonialism to corporations.

The Price Revolution / 价格革命

Between 1500 and 1650, European prices rose ~150%. The cause: massive silver and gold inflows from the Americas (Potosí, Zacatecas). This was the first documented case of inflation driven by money supply expansion — centuries before the quantity theory of money was formalized. Spain, the greatest beneficiary of American silver, paradoxically declined economically. Why? The Dutch disease avant la lettre: abundant silver made Spain a consumer rather than a producer. Spain imported goods; its own manufacturing withered.

Jean Bodin (1568) was the first to connect the silver influx to rising prices — anticipating the quantity theory of money. He observed that "the principal cause of the high prices is the abundance of gold and silver."
⚡ Politics / 政治

The State as Economic Actor / 国家作为经济主体

Mercantilism erased the distinction between state and economy. Jean-Baptiste Colbert, Louis XIV's finance minister, famously declared: "The art of taxation consists in so plucking the goose as to obtain the largest amount of feathers with the least possible amount of hissing." Colbert built France's navy, standardized manufacturing regulations, and promoted state-sponsored industries. The state was the economy's CEO.

The Dutch Exception / 荷兰例外

The Dutch Republic (1581–1795) was the anti-mercantilist mercantilist power. It had the world's largest merchant fleet, the first stock exchange (Amsterdam, 1602), the first central bank (Bank of Amsterdam, 1609), and relatively free trade. The Dutch demonstrated that commercial openness could generate more wealth than strict protectionism — a lesson England learned after the Glorious Revolution (1688), when Dutch financial expertise migrated to London.

The Triangle Trade / 三角贸易

The brutal economic engine of mercantilism: European manufactured goods → Africa (exchanged for enslaved people) → Americas (enslaved labor produced sugar, cotton, tobacco) → Europe. This wasn't a free market — it was a state-enforced, military-backed system of extraction. The wealth it generated funded the Industrial Revolution. Eric Williams' thesis (1944): British industrialization was financed substantially by the slave trade and Caribbean sugar profits.

📜 History / 历史
1492
Columbus reaches Americas
Opens the Columbian Exchange: potatoes, maize, tomatoes → Europe; wheat, sugar, horses, smallpox → Americas. Demographic and economic transformation of both hemispheres.
1494
Treaty of Tordesillas
Spain and Portugal divide the non-European world between them. The Pope arbitrates. Mercantilist colonialism begins in earnest.
1545
Potosí silver mine discovered
The world's richest silver deposit. By 1600, Potosí produced ~60% of the world's silver. Fueled the Spanish Empire — and European inflation.
1600 / 1602
East India Companies founded
English (1600) and Dutch (1602) East India Companies. The VOC was the world's first publicly traded company and first multinational corporation.
1637
Tulip Mania
The first documented speculative bubble. Tulip bulb futures prices collapsed. A lesson in crowd psychology — frequently misunderstood, but genuinely a bubble.
1651–1673
English Navigation Acts
Colonial trade restricted to English ships. Triggered three Anglo-Dutch Wars. Established England's maritime commercial dominance.
1720
South Sea Bubble
The South Sea Company's stock soared on speculation about its (nonexistent) trade profits. Crash ruined thousands. Isaac Newton lost £20,000 (~£3M today): "I can calculate the motions of heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people."
💰 Finance / 金融

The Birth of Public Finance / 公共财政的诞生

This period saw the invention of modern government finance. The Bank of England (1694) was founded to fund war against France — private investors lent £1.2M to the government at 8% and received banking privileges in return. This model — a privately owned central bank managing government debt — was revolutionary. It gave England a crucial advantage: the ability to borrow at rates far below its rivals, funding a navy that dominated global trade.

The VOC: First Multinational / 荷兰东印度公司

The Dutch East India Company (VOC) was the first corporation to issue publicly traded shares, pay regular dividends, and operate across multiple continents. It had its own army, minted its own coins, and negotiated treaties. At its peak, the VOC was worth ~$8 trillion in today's terms. The VOC was both a commercial enterprise and an instrument of state power — the blurring of corporation and empire that defines mercantilism.

The Amsterdam Stock Exchange (1602) and the Bank of Amsterdam (1609) created a financial infrastructure — public debt markets, tradable shares, central clearing, a stable unit of account — that England would copy and improve. The Dutch invented the tools; the British scaled them into global dominance.
📐 Mathematics / 数学

Quantity Theory of Money Emerges / 货币数量论出现

Bodin (1568): Prices ∝ Money Supply

Later formalized as: MV = PT
  M = money supply (silver/gold coin)
  V = velocity
  P = price level
  T = transactions

The Price Revolution (1500–1650):
  M ↑↑↑ (American silver) while T ↑ slowly
  → P ↑↑↑ (~150% over 150 years)

🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Zero-sum mentality / 零和心态: mercantilism's core fallacy — that trade is war by other means, one nation's gain = another's loss — persists in popular thinking about trade today. Adam Smith spent much of The Wealth of Nations arguing against this intuition, but it remains psychologically powerful because it's simpler than understanding comparative advantage.
  • Tulip and South Sea bubbles as psychology lessons: both bubbles followed the same pattern: excitement → mania → crash. Charles Mackay's 1841 Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds documented the recurring psychology of financial bubbles. The lesson: speculative manias are not aberrations but a recurring feature of human psychology interacting with novel financial instruments.
  • Colonial psychology / 殖民心理: the perception of colonies as natural extensions of national territory, with indigenous populations as obstacles rather than economic agents. The economic exploitation required psychological dehumanization — a pattern seen across empires from Rome to the Congo.
