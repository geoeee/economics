Exchange Rates / 汇率

The price of one currency in terms of another — and why this seemingly simple price governs trade flows, capital movements, and the limits of national economic policy.

📈 Economics / 经济学

Exchange Rate Regimes / 汇率制度

RegimeDescriptionExamples
Floating
浮动汇率		 Market-determined; central bank may intervene occasionally USD, EUR, JPY, GBP, AUD
Fixed (Pegged)
固定汇率		 Tied to another currency or basket at a set rate Saudi Riyal (to USD), Hong Kong Dollar (to USD)

管理浮动		 Mostly market-driven but central bank actively steers CNY, SGD, INR

货币局		 Strict fixed peg; domestic currency 100% backed by foreign reserves Hong Kong (until recently the purest example)

What Moves Exchange Rates? / 什么驱动汇率？

  • Interest rate differentials: higher rates attract capital inflows → currency appreciates
  • Inflation differentials: higher inflation erodes purchasing power → currency depreciates
  • Current account balance: persistent deficits → depreciation pressure (exports become cheaper)
  • Political stability / institutional quality: capital flees unstable countries
  • Speculation: FX is the world's largest market (~$7.5T/day); speculation dominates short-term moves

The Impossible Trinity / 不可能三角

A country can have at most TWO of these three:
① Free capital movement
② Fixed exchange rate
③ Independent monetary policy

Pick your poison: China chooses ②+③ (capital controls); Eurozone members choose ①+② (shared currency, no independent policy); US chooses ①+③ (floating rate).
⚡ Politics / 政治

Currency Manipulation / 汇率操纵

Countries can deliberately weaken their currency to boost exports — a beggar-thy-neighbor policy. The US Treasury regularly labels countries as "currency manipulators" (China, historically). But in a world of floating rates, defining "manipulation" is inherently political — every QE program also weakens a currency.

Reserve Currency Status / 储备货币地位

The US dollar is the world's dominant reserve currency (~59% of global reserves). This grants the US exorbitant privilege — the ability to borrow in its own currency, run persistent deficits, and exert financial power through dollar dominance (sanctions, SWIFT access). The de-dollarization debate — whether the yuan, euro, or crypto can displace the dollar — is one of the defining geopolitical questions of this era.

📜 History / 历史
1870–1914
Classical Gold Standard
Currencies fixed to gold at set rates. Automatic adjustment mechanism: trade deficits → gold outflow → money supply shrinks → prices fall → exports become competitive. Harsh but self-correcting.
1944–1971
Bretton Woods System
Dollar pegged to gold ($35/oz); all other currencies pegged to dollar (adjustable). Created stability but US couldn't maintain gold convertibility as dollars piled up abroad (Triffin dilemma).
1971–present
Fiat Floating Era
Nixon closed the gold window. Most major currencies floated. Exchange rates became market-determined. Volatility increased; so did policy flexibility.
1992
Black Wednesday
George Soros "broke the Bank of England" — shorting the pound forced the UK out of the ERM. Demonstrated that markets can overwhelm central banks defending a peg.
1997
Asian Financial Crisis
Fixed-but-adjustable pegs collapsed under speculative attacks across Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea. Lesson: pegs are dangerous in a world of mobile capital.
💰 Finance / 金融

FX is the world's most liquid market. Carry trade: borrow in low-rate currency (JPY), invest in high-rate currency (AUD, BRL) — profit from the interest differential as long as exchange rates don't move against you. When they do (sharp yen appreciation), the unwinding creates violent moves (2008, 2024).

Purchasing Power Parity (PPP): in the long run, exchange rates should adjust so that identical goods cost the same across countries. The Big Mac Index (The Economist) is a popular informal PPP measure. In reality, PPP holds poorly — deviations are large and persistent, because not all goods are tradeable (haircuts, rent) and capital flows dominate.

📐 Mathematics / 数学

Covered Interest Rate Parity / 抵补利率平价

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{F}{S} &= \frac{1 + i_{\text{domestic}}}{1 + i_{\text{foreign}}} \\[4pt] F &= \text{forward exchange rate} \\ S &= \text{spot exchange rate} \\ &\text{If this doesn't hold } \rightarrow \text{ risk-free arbitrage exists} \end{aligned}$$

Real Exchange Rate / 实际汇率

$$\begin{aligned} \text{RER} &= E \times \frac{P_{\text{foreign}}}{P_{\text{domestic}}} \\[4pt] E &= \text{nominal exchange rate} \\ \text{RER} > 1 &\rightarrow \text{ domestic goods relatively expensive (overvalued)} \\ \text{RER} < 1 &\rightarrow \text{ domestic goods relatively cheap (undervalued)} \end{aligned}$$

Balance of Payments / 国际收支

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Current Account} + \text{Capital Account} + \text{Financial Account} &= 0 \\[4pt] \text{CA deficit} &= \text{Capital} + \text{Financial Account surplus} \\ &\text{A country must borrow (or sell assets) to run a trade deficit} \end{aligned}$$
🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Home currency bias / 本币偏好: investors disproportionately hold domestic assets despite diversification benefits. This creates persistent demand for the domestic currency, contributing to exchange rate deviations from fundamentals.
  • Exchange rate as national virility / 汇率即国威: a strong currency is psychologically perceived as a strong nation. Politicians celebrate appreciation and panic at depreciation — even though a weaker currency helps exporters. This is the exchange rate prestige illusion.
  • Round number effects / 整数效应: currency pairs tend to cluster at round numbers (EUR/USD at 1.00, USD/JPY at 150). These psychological levels become support/resistance — purely because traders believe others believe they matter.
