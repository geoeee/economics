Supply & Demand / 供需关系

The most fundamental model in economics: how prices emerge from the interaction of what producers are willing to sell and what consumers are willing to buy.

📈 Economics / 经济学

The Law of Demand / 需求定律

As price rises, quantity demanded falls — all else equal (ceteris paribus). The demand curve slopes downward because of the substitution effect (consumers switch to cheaper alternatives) and the income effect (higher prices reduce real purchasing power).

The Law of Supply / 供给定律

As price rises, quantity supplied rises. Producers have incentive to produce more when prices are higher. The supply curve slopes upward, reflecting increasing marginal cost — producing more eventually becomes more expensive per unit.

Equilibrium / 均衡

The intersection of supply and demand curves is the market equilibrium — a price where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. At any other price, surpluses (excess supply) or shortages (excess demand) create pressure toward equilibrium.

Elasticity / 弹性

Price elasticity of demand measures how responsive quantity demanded is to price changes. Elastic goods (luxuries, many substitutes) see large quantity changes; inelastic goods (necessities, addictive products) see small changes. Cross-price elasticity reveals whether goods are substitutes or complements.

Demand shifters Income, preferences, prices of related goods, expectations, number of buyers
Supply shifters Input costs, technology, taxes/subsidies, number of sellers, expectations
⚡ Politics / 政治

Price Controls / 价格管制

Governments often intervene in markets through price ceilings (maximum prices, e.g. rent control) and price floors (minimum prices, e.g. minimum wage). While well-intentioned, price controls create predictable problems:

  • Ceilings → shortages: below-equilibrium prices cause excess demand (queuing, black markets)
  • Floors → surpluses: above-equilibrium prices cause excess supply (unsold goods, unemployment)
Rent control is the most efficient way to destroy a city's housing supply — except for bombing.
— Attributed to various economists

Taxes and Deadweight Loss / 税收与无谓损失

Taxes drive a wedge between the price buyers pay and sellers receive, reducing the equilibrium quantity. The resulting deadweight loss is value destroyed — trades that would benefit both parties but don't happen because of the tax. Who bears the tax burden (tax incidence) depends on relative elasticities, not on who the tax is nominally levied on.

📜 History / 历史

The supply-demand framework was formalized by Alfred Marshall in Principles of Economics (1890), who introduced the famous "scissors" analogy — asking whether supply or demand determines price is like asking which blade of scissors cuts paper. Both do, simultaneously.

Earlier economists (Adam Smith, David Ricardo) had described price formation, but Marshall's contribution was the marginal analysis of both sides, the concept of elasticity, and the clear graphical representation we still use today.

The framework's real-world limits became apparent during the Great Depression, when massive unemployment coexisted with unused factories — supply and demand failed to self-correct, inspiring Keynes to develop macroeconomics.

💰 Finance / 金融

Financial markets are the purest real-world expression of supply and demand. Order books display bid (demand) and ask (supply) prices in real time. The bid-ask spread is a direct measure of market liquidity — tight spreads indicate high volumes of willing buyers and sellers.

In bond markets, demand for government debt determines yields (inverse to price). Central banks manipulate supply (QE purchases) and demand (interest rate signals) to influence the entire yield curve. The supply of money interacts with the demand for money to determine interest rates in the economy.

📐 Mathematics / 数学

Linear Model / 线性模型

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Demand:}\quad Q_d &= a - bP &&\text{(a: intercept, b: slope)} \\ \text{Supply:}\quad Q_s &= c + dP &&\text{(c: intercept, d: slope)} \\[4pt] \text{Equilibrium:}\quad Q_d &= Q_s \;\rightarrow\; P^* = \frac{a - c}{b + d} \end{aligned}$$

Elasticity Formula / 弹性公式

$$\begin{aligned} &\text{Price Elasticity of Demand:} \\ E_d &= \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \frac{P}{Q} \\[4pt] |E_d| > 1 \;&\rightarrow\; \text{Elastic (luxury goods)} \\ |E_d| < 1 \;&\rightarrow\; \text{Inelastic (necessities)} \\ |E_d| = 1 \;&\rightarrow\; \text{Unit elastic} \end{aligned}$$

Consumer & Producer Surplus / 消费者与生产者剩余

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Consumer Surplus} &= \int_{0}^{Q^*} D(Q)\,dQ - P^* \times Q^* \\ \text{Producer Surplus} &= P^* \times Q^* - \int_{0}^{Q^*} S(Q)\,dQ \\[4pt] \text{Total Welfare} &= CS + PS \\ \text{Deadweight Loss} &= \text{lost surplus from underproduction} \end{aligned}$$
🧠 Psychology / 心理

The standard model assumes rational actors, but behavioral economics reveals systematic deviations:

  • Anchoring: the first price you see becomes a reference point, distorting your perception of value. This is why stores show "original" prices crossed out.
  • Framing effects: "90% fat-free" sells better than "10% fat" — same information, different demand response.
  • Giffen goods: theoretically, goods for which demand increases as price rises (poverty-linked staples). The Irish potato famine provides contested evidence — as potato prices rose, the poor bought more potatoes because they could no longer afford meat.
  • Veblen goods: luxury items where higher prices increase demand due to status signaling (conspicuous consumption).

These exceptions don't invalidate supply-demand logic; they reveal that demand curves aren't always well-behaved downward slopes.
