Neoliberal Era / 新自由主义时代

The pendulum swings: from managed capitalism back to free markets. Fiat money, financial deregulation, globalization, and the rise of China — the era that remade the global economy and set the stage for 2008.

📈 Economics / 经济学

The Monetarist Counter-Revolution / 货币主义反革命

Milton Friedman led the intellectual assault on Keynesianism. Core arguments: inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon — caused by excessive money supply growth, not by oil shocks or unions. The natural rate of unemployment (NAIRU) meant there was no long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment. Attempting to push unemployment below the natural rate would only cause accelerating inflation. The policy prescription: central banks should target steady, moderate money supply growth — not attempt to fine-tune the economy. Friedman's critique was vindicated by the stagflation of the 1970s, which Keynesian models couldn't explain.

The Washington Consensus / 华盛顿共识

John Williamson (1989) coined the term for the policy package promoted by the IMF, World Bank, and US Treasury for developing countries: fiscal discipline, tax reform, trade liberalization, privatization, deregulation, secure property rights. The "Consensus" was applied across Latin America, post-Soviet states, and developing Asia — with deeply mixed results. Where it worked (Chile, parts of East Asia), growth followed. Where it failed (Russia's "shock therapy," Argentina 2001), the results were catastrophic. The debate over whether the Consensus was good economics poorly applied, or bad economics that sometimes worked by accident, continues.

The Rise of China / 中国的崛起

Deng Xiaoping's reforms (from 1978) created a unique hybrid: a Communist Party maintaining political control while progressively marketizing the economy. China's GDP grew at ~10% annually for three decades — the fastest sustained growth in human history. By 2010, China passed Japan as the world's second-largest economy; by 2014 (PPP), it passed the US as the largest. China's success was deeply awkward for the Washington Consensus: it succeeded by doing almost everything the Consensus said not to do — maintaining state-owned enterprises, using industrial policy, controlling capital flows, and liberalizing gradually rather than through "shock therapy."

The neoliberal era saw the return to pre-1914 levels of global economic integration (trade/GDP, capital flows/GDP) — but with a crucial difference: this time, currencies floated, giving countries more flexibility. Or so the theory said. In practice, the rise of global finance created new vulnerabilities.
⚡ Politics / 政治

Reagan and Thatcher / 里根与撒切尔

Margaret Thatcher (UK PM 1979–1990) and Ronald Reagan (US President 1981–1989) were the political faces of the neoliberal turn. Their shared program: cut taxes (especially on high earners), deregulate industries, privatize state-owned enterprises, weaken unions, and prioritize inflation control over full employment. Thatcher's maxim — "there is no alternative" (TINA) — captured the era's ideological confidence. Both confronted and defeated major strikes (UK miners 1984–85, US air traffic controllers 1981), breaking the union power that had constrained capital since the 1930s.

The End of History? / 历史的终结？

The fall of the Berlin Wall (1989) and Soviet Union (1991) seemed to vindicate capitalism definitively. Francis Fukuyama's "End of History" thesis argued liberal democracy and market economics had won — the fundamental ideological debates were over. The 1990s were the decade of triumphalism: NAFTA, WTO, EU expansion, "New Economy" optimism. In retrospect, the triumphalism was premature — the inequalities, instabilities, and backlashes that would define the 21st century were already building.

Globalization and Its Discontents / 全球化及其不满

The anti-globalization movement erupted in Seattle (1999 WTO protests) and grew through the 2000s. Critics argued that free trade agreements prioritized corporate interests over labor and environmental standards, that the IMF and World Bank imposed harmful austerity, and that globalization created a "race to the bottom" in wages and regulations. The 2008 crisis, followed by Brexit and Trump (both 2016), transformed these critiques from marginal protests into the dominant political force in the West.

📜 History / 历史
1971
Nixon Shock
Gold window closed. Fixed exchange rates ended. Currencies floated. The fiat money era begins.
1973, 1979
Oil Shocks
OPEC embargo → oil price quadrupled (1973). Iranian Revolution → oil price doubled (1979). Stagflation in the West. Petrodollar recycling system created.
1979–1982
Volcker Shock
Fed Chair Paul Volcker raised rates to ~20% to crush inflation. Caused deep recession. Succeeded. Defined the central banker as inflation-fighter.
1989–1991
Fall of Communism
Berlin Wall falls. Soviet Union dissolves. ~2 billion people shift from planned to market economies. Capitalist triumphalism peaks.
1992
EU Single Market & Maastricht Treaty
Euro launched (1999 as accounting currency, 2002 as cash). ECB created. Monetary union without fiscal union.
1997
Asian Financial Crisis
Fixed pegs collapsed. Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea devastated. IMF austerity deepened the crisis. Lesson: open capital accounts are dangerous.
2001
China Joins WTO
Integrating China into global trade: the largest economic event of the late 20th century. Container ships, offshoring, cheap goods — and deindustrialization in the West.
2001
Dot-com Bubble Bursts
NASDAQ fell ~78%. ~$5T in market value destroyed. But the internet infrastructure survived. E-commerce, cloud, social media rose from the ashes.
💰 Finance / 金融

The Great Financialization / 大金融化

The neoliberal era saw finance grow from a servant of industry to the dominant sector of the economy. US financial sector profits rose from ~10% of corporate profits in the 1970s to ~40% by the 2000s. Key drivers:

  • Deregulation: repeal of Glass-Steagall (1999, US), Big Bang (1986, UK) — removing barriers between commercial and investment banking
  • Securitization: bundling loans into tradable securities. Mortgages, credit card debt, student loans — anything with a cash flow could be securitized. Spread risk, or concentrated it? Both.
  • Shadow banking: lending by non-banks (money market funds, hedge funds, SIVs) grew to rival traditional banking. Created credit outside regulatory oversight — the epicenter of the 2008 crisis
  • Global imbalances: China and oil exporters accumulated massive dollar reserves, which were recycled into US assets, pushing down interest rates and fueling the credit boom
Between 1980 and 2007, the global financial asset-to-GDP ratio roughly tripled. Finance was no longer intermediating between savers and investors — it was increasingly trading with itself, generating fees from complexity. The "real economy" and "financial economy" had decoupled.
📐 Mathematics / 数学

Friedman's k-Percent Rule / 弗里德曼的固定货币增长率

ΔM = k% annually (Friedman proposed 3–5%)

Rationale:
  MV = PY
  If V stable, Y grows ~3%, then ΔM = 3% → stable P
  Avoids central bank discretion (which Friedman distrusted)

Never fully adopted; replaced by inflation targeting
by the 1990s. But the logic — rules beat discretion —
remains influential

Efficient Market Hypothesis / 有效市场假说

P_t = E[P_t | all available information at t−1]

Prices reflect all available information.
You cannot consistently beat the market.
Bubbles are, by definition, impossible.

The 2008 crisis demonstrated that markets are
not, in fact, always efficient. But the EMH remains
the dominant framework in academic finance —
with "behavioral" caveats added.
🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • "Greed is good" / 贪婪是美德: Gordon Gekko's 1987 speech captured the psychological shift of the era. After decades of post-war restraint, the neoliberal era celebrated self-interest, entrepreneurship, and wealth accumulation. The cultural narrative shifted from "we're all in this together" to "you're on your own."
  • The psychology of bubbles in the information age: the dot-com bubble (1995–2001) was the first internet-era speculative mania. CNBC, day-trading platforms, and online forums accelerated the spread of hype — and panic. The combination of genuinely transformative technology + speculative frenzy produced a classic bubble with modern speed.
  • Housing as the American Dream / 住房即美国梦: the psychological centrality of homeownership in American identity made housing the perfect vehicle for speculative excess. "House prices always go up" was both empirically false and psychologically irresistible. The 2008 crisis was a housing bubble blown up by financial engineering — but its psychological roots were in the deep-seated American belief that owning a home was the path to security and status.
