Industrial Revolution / 工业革命

The century that shattered every economic assumption: sustained growth replaced cycles, factories replaced farms, and two thinkers — Adam Smith and Karl Marx — defined the debate over capitalism that continues today.

📈 Economics / 经济学

Adam Smith: The Wealth of Nations (1776) / 《国富论》

Published the same year as the American Declaration of Independence, Smith's Wealth of Nations founded modern economics. Its core arguments:

  • The division of labor: Smith's pin factory example — 10 workers specializing can produce 48,000 pins/day; working individually, maybe 200. Specialization → productivity → wealth
  • The invisible hand: individuals pursuing self-interest in competitive markets are "led by an invisible hand" to promote social welfare — not through benevolence, but through the market mechanism. The baker doesn't bake for you out of kindness; he bakes because you pay
  • Free trade: mercantilism is wrong. Trade is positive-sum. Comparative advantage (developed later by Ricardo) means both sides gain
  • The labor theory of value: the "real price" of a good is the labor required to produce it. Smith used this inconsistently; later economists (Ricardo, Marx) extended it, and the "marginal revolution" (1870s) replaced it

Classical Economics / 古典经济学

The century after Smith produced a flourishing of systematic economic thought: Thomas Malthus (population grows geometrically, food arithmetically → inevitable poverty — wrong because he missed technological progress), David Ricardo (diminishing returns to land, comparative advantage, the theory of rent), and Jean-Baptiste Say (Say's Law: "supply creates its own demand" — disputed by Keynes a century later). By 1848, John Stuart Mill's Principles of Political Economy synthesized classical economics into a comprehensive system.

Marx's Critique of Capitalism / 马克思对资本主义的批判

Das Kapital (1867): capitalism's surface (voluntary exchange in free markets) hides exploitation. Capitalists own the means of production; workers own only their labor power, which they must sell to survive. The difference between the value workers create and the wages they receive is surplus value — the source of profit and the engine of capital accumulation. Marx predicted capitalism would self-destruct through falling profit rates, increasing immiseration of workers, and increasingly severe crises. He was wrong about immiseration (real wages rose dramatically under capitalism), largely right about instability, and his analysis of class dynamics remains influential.

⚡ Politics / 政治

The Factory Acts / 工厂法

Industrialization concentrated workers in factories under brutal conditions — 14-hour days, child labor, dangerous machinery. The Factory Acts (1802–1878 in Britain) were the first systematic government regulation of working conditions: limiting child labor, mandating education, setting maximum hours. They were bitterly opposed by industrialists who argued they would destroy British competitiveness — an argument that echoes in every subsequent labor regulation debate.

Corn Laws and Free Trade / 《谷物法》与自由贸易

The Corn Laws (1815–1846) restricted grain imports to protect British landowners — raising food prices for workers and industrialists alike. The Anti-Corn Law League, led by Richard Cobden, mobilized a mass political movement that finally won repeal in 1846. This was a watershed: the shift from protectionism (favoring landed aristocracy) toward free trade (favoring industrialists and workers). Britain wouldn't return to protectionism until the 20th century.

Marxism as Political Movement / 马克思主义作为政治运动

Marx's ideas didn't stay in libraries. The Communist Manifesto (1848) called for workers of the world to unite. The first socialist and communist parties formed. The Paris Commune (1871) briefly established revolutionary government. Marx's ideas — filtered through Lenin, Mao, and others — would eventually govern half the world's population in the 20th century.

💰 Finance / 金融

Financing the Revolution / 为工业革命融资

Early factories were surprisingly cheap — a textile mill cost ~£5,000, fundable through family and local partnerships. Real financial innovation was needed for infrastructure: canals, then railways. The joint-stock company (with limited liability after 1855 in Britain) allowed thousands of investors to pool capital for projects far beyond any individual's wealth. The London Stock Exchange grew from a coffee house to a global financial center. Bank of England notes became a trusted national currency — a critical infrastructure for a market economy.

Bubbles and Crises / 泡沫与危机

The industrial era brought a new phenomenon: the modern business cycle — not just agricultural fluctuations but investment-led booms and busts. Canal mania (1790s), Latin American bond mania (1820s), and railway mania (1840s) all followed the same pattern: excitement about a transformative technology → capital floods in → overbuilding → crash → consolidation. The railway was the original "tech bubble" — real transformative potential combined with wild over-investment and inevitable bust.

Ricardo's Comparative Advantage / 李嘉图的比较优势

                Wine    Cloth  (labor hours per unit)
England         120      100
Portugal        80       90

Portugal is better at BOTH (absolute advantage)
But:
  Portugal better at wine by 40h, cloth by 10h
  → Portugal's comparative advantage: wine
  → England's comparative advantage: cloth

Trade benefits both even though Portugal is
absolutely better at everything

Malthusian Model / 马尔萨斯模型

Population: P(t) = P₀ × 2^(t/T)  (geometric)
Food:       F(t) = F₀ + a×t         (arithmetic)

Inevitable crisis when P(t) > F(t)
→ famine, disease, war → population falls
→ cycle resets

Prediction failed because Malthus missed:
  technological progress in agriculture
  demographic transition (wealth → fewer children)
🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Factory discipline / 工厂纪律: pre-industrial workers controlled their own pace. Factory work required showing up at a fixed time, working at machine pace, and accepting hierarchy. This required a psychological transformation — what E.P. Thompson called a shift from "task-orientation" to "time-orientation." The clock became the instrument of discipline.
  • The alienation of labor / 劳动异化: Marx's concept that factory work estranges workers from their productive activity (they don't control it), from the product (they don't own it), from their human potential (work becomes merely a means to survive), and from other workers (competition replaces cooperation). This was simultaneously an economic analysis and a psychological diagnosis.
  • Smithian sympathy / 斯密式共情: Smith's first book (The Theory of Moral Sentiments, 1759) was about human psychology — our capacity for empathy and the "impartial spectator" within. Smith argued that markets work not because people are selfish, but because they possess sympathy — the psychological foundation of trust that makes exchange possible.
