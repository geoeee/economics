Bretton Woods / 战后秩序

The "golden age of capitalism" — unprecedented growth, full employment, and expanding welfare states, all built on a US-led international economic order that lasted exactly one generation before its internal contradictions tore it apart.

📈 Economics / 经济学

The Bretton Woods System / 布雷顿森林体系

Designed by Keynes and Harry Dexter White at the 1944 conference, Bretton Woods was a compromise: it preserved the stability of fixed exchange rates (like the gold standard) while giving countries policy autonomy (unlike the gold standard). The mechanism: currencies were pegged to the dollar at adjustable rates; the dollar was pegged to gold at $35/oz. Countries could adjust their peg in cases of "fundamental disequilibrium." Capital controls prevented speculative attacks. It was a system designed by people who had lived through the Depression and understood that free capital flows + fixed rates + domestic policy autonomy couldn't all coexist.

The Keynesian Golden Age / 凯恩斯主义黄金时代

From ~1945 to ~1973, the developed world experienced the fastest sustained economic growth in history. French call it Les Trente Glorieuses (the Glorious Thirty). Japanese GDP grew at ~9% annually. Western Europe grew at ~5%. Unemployment in most developed countries stayed below 3%. This was "managed capitalism" — governments used fiscal and monetary policy to maintain full employment, built welfare states, and regulated finance. The synthesis of capitalism and social democracy seemed permanent.

This suggested a policy trade-off: lower unemployment = higher inflation. Policymakers could choose their preferred point on the curve. By the late 1960s, the trade-off broke down — both inflation and unemployment rose simultaneously (stagflation), and the Phillips Curve became the most famously wrong empirical regularity in economics.

⚡ Politics / 政治

Embedded Liberalism / 嵌入式自由主义

Political scientist John Ruggie coined this term: the post-war order "embedded" free markets within political constraints. Unlike the 19th-century gold standard (where domestic policy served exchange rate stability), the Bretton Woods system subordinated international financial stability to domestic goals — full employment, welfare, growth. This was the great political-economic bargain of the era: accept market exchange internationally, but cushion its domestic effects.

Cold War Economics / 冷战经济学

The Cold War was an economic competition as much as a military one. The US used economic aid (Marshall Plan, $13B → Western Europe, 1948–1952) and trade access to bind allies into its system. The Soviet Union responded with Comecon — its own economic bloc. The competition extended to development: which system (capitalism or communism) could lift poor countries out of poverty? Both sides poured money into client states. The outcomes were mixed — but by the 1980s, the answer was becoming clear.

The Marshall Plan / 马歇尔计划

Perhaps the most successful foreign aid program in history. $13 billion (~$150B in 2024 dollars) to rebuild Western Europe. But it wasn't just charity — it was enlightened self-interest: prosperous trading partners = prosperous US. The Marshall Plan required European cooperation (a seed of the EU), opened European markets to US goods, and bound Western Europe firmly to the American-led order.

💰 Finance / 金融

Financial Repression / 金融抑制

金融抑制的时代：政府将利率保持在低位（低于通胀水平），实施资本管制，并将信贷引导到优先部门。这是有意为之的政策——它使政府能够廉价地减少巨额的二战债务（通过通胀将其稀释），同时将资本引向工业发展。储户受损（实际回报为负），但经济增长得足够快，生活水平仍然提高了。">The post-war era was one of financial repression: governments kept interest rates low (below inflation), imposed capital controls, and directed credit to priority sectors. This was deliberate policy — it allowed governments to reduce their massive WWII debts cheaply (by inflating them away) while directing capital toward industrial development. Savers lost (negative real returns), but economies grew fast enough that living standards still rose.

Eurodollar Market / 欧元市场

欧洲美元市场是现代全球金融的雏形——资金在没有任何单一政府控制的情况下跨境流动。到1960年代，这个市场的规模已经大到足以制约美国的货币政策。">A crucial unintended consequence: dollars held outside the US (initially by Soviet oil exporters who didn't trust US banks) created an unregulated offshore financial market in London. The Eurodollar market was the seed of modern global finance — money moving across borders outside the control of any single government. By the 1960s, this market was large enough to constrain US monetary policy.

The Triffin Dilemma / 特里芬难题

Robert Triffin identified Bretton Woods' fatal flaw in 1960: the system required the US to supply dollars to the world (by running trade deficits), but the more dollars accumulated abroad, the less credible the $35/oz gold promise became. By 1971, foreign dollar holdings exceeded US gold reserves by ~4:1. When France and others demanded gold for dollars, Nixon had no choice but to close the window. Bretton Woods was designed with an expiry date built in.

🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Post-war optimism / 战后乐观主义: the generation that survived depression and war genuinely believed they could build a better world. Bretton Woods, the UN, the welfare state, decolonization — all were products of the conviction that institutions could master economic forces. This psychological confidence enabled the construction of the post-war order.
  • Inflation psychology shifts / 通胀心理转变: in the 1960s, policymakers genuinely believed they could fine-tune the economy to achieve both low unemployment and low inflation. This overconfidence — the "age of hubris" — led to the inflationary policies that triggered stagflation. The lesson that economics is less controllable than we think came painfully.
  • The American Dream as economic narrative: the post-war period saw the construction of the "American Dream" — the belief that each generation would live better than the last. For ~25 years, this was empirically true. When growth slowed after 1973, the gap between expectation and reality became a source of political discontent that continues today.
