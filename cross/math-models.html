📈 Economics / 经济学

Why Math in Economics?

Economics is the study of constrained optimization — how agents maximize utility or profit given limited resources. Mathematics provides precision where words are ambiguous, allows testing theories against data, and reveals insights that intuition alone cannot reach (e.g. comparative advantage, deadweight loss of taxation, the impossibility of a social welfare function).

Paul Samuelson, who mathematized economics with his 1947 Foundations of Economic Analysis, argued that economics and physics share a common mathematical structure — both study equilibrium systems responding to shocks.

⚡ Politics / 政治

Models as Political Weapons / 模型作为政治武器

Economic models are never purely technical — they embed assumptions that have political implications. A model assuming perfect competition suggests deregulation; a model of market failure justifies intervention. The choice of which model to use is itself a political act.

  • DSGE models (Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium): used by central banks, criticized for failing to predict the 2008 crisis. Assume rational expectations and efficient markets.
  • Input-output models: Wassily Leontief (Nobel 1973) developed this for planning. Used extensively by the USSR and China's NDRC — a mathematical basis for state-guided development.
  • Climate models (IAMs — Integrated Assessment Models): the choice of discount rate (how much to value future generations) is a political-philosophical decision masquerading as a technical parameter. Stern (1.4%) vs Nordhaus (4.3%) produce radically different policy conclusions.
📜 History / 历史
1838
Cournot: First use of calculus in economics
Applied differential calculus to model firm profit maximization. Foundation of mathematical economics.
1870s
Marginal Revolution
Jevons, Menger, Walras independently developed marginal utility theory — calculus becomes essential to economics.
1930s–40s
Econometrics & Input-Output
Tinbergen, Frisch, Leontief, Koopmans: statistical methods for testing economic theories. Linear algebra meets economics.
1947
Samuelson's Foundations
Unified economics under constrained optimization. "Maximum principles" — all economic behavior as maximizing something subject to constraints.
1970s–80s
Rational Expectations Revolution
Lucas, Sargent: agents optimize using all available information. DSGE models become central bank workhorses.
2000s–present
Machine Learning & Big Data
Causal inference (Angrist, Imbens), random forests for demand estimation, NLP on central bank communications.
💰 Finance / 金融
  • Markowitz Portfolio Theory (1952): mean-variance optimization. The efficient frontier: maximize expected return for a given level of risk. Covariance matrices — linear algebra is the math of diversification.
  • Black-Scholes-Merton (1973): partial differential equation for option pricing. Nobel Prize 1997. Underpinned the explosion of derivatives markets — and the 2008 crisis, when models failed to account for tail risk.
  • Value at Risk (VaR): "What's the most I can lose in a bad day?" Widely used and widely criticized — VaR assumes normal distributions and independence, both of which fail catastrophically in crises.
  • Monte Carlo simulation: running thousands of random scenarios to price complex derivatives or assess portfolio risk. Computationally intensive but conceptually simple.
📐 Mathematics / 数学

Calculus: Marginal Analysis / 边际分析

Total Revenue: TR = P(Q) × Q
Marginal Revenue: MR = d(TR)/dQ
Profit max: MR = MC

Elasticity: ε = (dQ/dP) × (P/Q)

Optimization: Lagrange Multipliers / 拉格朗日乘数

Maximize utility u(x,y) subject to budget p_x·x + p_y·y ≤ M

ℒ = u(x,y) − λ(p_x·x + p_y·y − M)

∂ℒ/∂x = 0, ∂ℒ/∂y = 0 → MU_x/p_x = MU_y/p_y = λ

λ = marginal utility of income (shadow price of the constraint)

Linear Algebra: Input-Output / 投入产出

x = Ax + d → x = (I − A)^(−1)d

x = total output vector
A = technical coefficient matrix
d = final demand vector
(I − A)^(−1) = Leontief inverse matrix

Regression / 回归分析

y_i = β₀ + β₁x_i + ε_i

β₁ = Cov(x,y) / Var(x) = Σ(x_i−x̄)(y_i−ȳ) / Σ(x_i−x̄)²

R² = 1 − (SS_res / SS_tot)  — fraction of variance explained
Causal interpretation requires no omitted variable bias

Differential Equations: Solow Growth / 索洛增长

dk/dt = s·f(k) − (n + δ)·k

k = capital per worker
s = savings rate
f(k) = production function per worker
n = population growth rate
δ = depreciation rate

Steady state: s·f(k*) = (n + δ)·k*
🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Math anxiety / 数学焦虑: many people's aversion to mathematical economics is psychological, not intellectual. The concepts are often intuitive; the notation creates a barrier. Good economists translate between mathematical precision and human intuition.
  • Over-mathematization critique / 过度数学化批评: economists have been accused of "physics envy" — preferring elegant math over messy reality. The 2008 crisis exposed the danger: models assumed away the possibility of systemic collapse because it was mathematically inconvenient.
  • Model certainty vs real uncertainty: Knightian uncertainty (unknowable probabilities, Frank Knight 1921) vs calculable risk. Math handles risk beautifully; it handles fundamental uncertainty poorly. The most important economic questions often lie in the latter category.
