World Wars & Depression / 战争与萧条

Three decades of catastrophe — two world wars and the Great Depression — that destroyed the old economic order and gave birth to modern macroeconomics, the welfare state, and the conviction that governments must manage their economies.

📈 Economics / 经济学

The Great Depression: What Happened? / 大萧条

Between 1929 and 1933, US GDP fell ~30%, unemployment hit 25%, and ~9,000 banks failed. Industrial production halved. The Depression spread globally — Germany's unemployment hit ~30% by 1932, directly enabling Hitler's rise. The gold standard was a key transmission mechanism: when the US contracted, it pulled gold from other countries, forcing them to contract too. Countries that abandoned the gold standard earliest (UK 1931, Japan 1931) recovered soonest. The US (stayed on gold until 1933) recovered latest.

Keynes and the General Theory (1936) / 凯恩斯与《通论》

Keynes' The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money was a devastating response to classical economics. Core arguments:

  • Demand, not supply, determines output: recessions are caused by insufficient aggregate demand, not by supply-side problems. Workers are unemployed because there aren't enough buyers for what they could produce — not because wages are "too high."
  • Saving is not always virtuous: the paradox of thrift — if everyone tries to save more during a recession, aggregate demand falls further, income falls, and total saving actually decreases.
  • Government must manage demand: when private spending collapses, the government must spend to fill the gap. Fiscal stimulus is not optional — it's the only way out when monetary policy is impotent (the "liquidity trap").
  • Animal spirits: investment decisions are driven by psychology, not just rational calculation. The "state of confidence" is fragile and can collapse without cause.
Keynes was asked if he could think of anything that had ever contradicted his theories. His reply: "Yes, in the long run we are all dead." The classical argument that markets eventually self-correct was technically true — but "eventually" might mean decades of mass unemployment.

The New Deal / 罗斯福新政

FDR's New Deal (1933–1939) was the first large-scale experiment in Keynesian demand management — though Keynes himself criticized it as too timid. It included: bank reform (FDIC insurance), public works (WPA, CCC), Social Security, and labor rights (Wagner Act). Whether the New Deal "ended" the Depression is contested — it reduced unemployment from 25% to ~14%, but full recovery only came with WWII spending. The real lesson: fiscal stimulus on the scale of war mobilization definitely works.

⚡ Politics / 政治

War Economies / 战争经济

WWI and WWII transformed the relationship between state and economy. In WWI, governments took control of production, allocated resources, and ran massive deficits — discovering, accidentally, that sovereign states could mobilize economies at scales previously unimaginable. WWII was even more extreme: the US ran deficits of ~25% of GDP, imposed wage and price controls, and directed industrial production. The US economy grew ~75% between 1941–1945 — proving that an economy operating far below capacity (post-Depression) could grow explosively with sufficient demand.

Weimar Hyperinflation / 魏玛恶性通胀

Germany 1923: prices doubled every ~3.7 days. A loaf of bread cost 200 billion marks. The cause was not "printing money for no reason" — Germany printed money to pay war reparations after its economy was devastated. The hyperinflation destroyed the middle class's savings, eroded faith in democracy, and created a traumatized population susceptible to extremism. The lesson: hyperinflation causes political catastrophe, not just economic hardship.

Depression and Democracy / 萧条与民主

The Great Depression killed democracies. Germany's Weimar Republic fell to Hitler (1933). Austria, Poland, Hungary, and others fell to authoritarianism. Where democracy survived (US, UK, Scandinavia), it was because governments responded — the New Deal, the welfare state — showing that democratic capitalism could deliver. The Depression taught a brutal lesson: sustained economic misery will destroy political systems.

1939–1945
World War II
War spending ended the Depression. US GDP doubled. Bretton Woods conference (1944) planned the post-war economic order.
💰 Finance / 金融

Bank Failures and the Fed's Failure / 银行倒闭与美联储的失败

Between 1929 and 1933, ~9,000 US banks failed — about one-third of all banks. The Federal Reserve, created to prevent exactly this, failed catastrophically. Why? The Fed believed that failing banks were "weak" and should be allowed to fail — a liquidationist view. The Fed also raised interest rates in 1931 to defend the gold standard, contracting the money supply by ~30%. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz's A Monetary History of the United States (1963) demonstrated that the Depression was caused by this catastrophic monetary contraction — not by an inherent flaw in capitalism.

When bank failures wiped out depositors' savings, the money supply imploded. People hoarded cash, spending collapsed, and the real economy followed. The Depression was a financial crisis that became an economic catastrophe because the central bank failed its most basic function: being a lender of last resort.

Bretton Woods: Designing the Future / 设计未来

In July 1944, while WWII still raged, 730 delegates from 44 nations met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design the post-war economic order. The result: the IMF, the World Bank, and a system of fixed-but-adjustable exchange rates pegged to the dollar, which was pegged to gold ($35/oz). This was Keynes's last great project — though his more ambitious plan for an international currency (the "bancor") was rejected in favor of US dollar dominance.

📐 Mathematics / 数学

The Multiplier Effect / 乘数效应

ΔY = k × ΔG

k = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / MPS

MPC = marginal propensity to consume
MPS = marginal propensity to save

If MPC = 0.8, k = 5
→ $1B government spending → $5B GDP increase

Keynes' key insight: modest government spending
can have outsized effects on output

Liquidity Trap / 流动性陷阱

When interest rates near zero:
  Money demand becomes perfectly elastic
  → more money supply doesn't lower rates
  → monetary policy becomes impotent

Solution: fiscal policy (government spending)
must directly create demand

GDP Calculation (Invented in this era)

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

Kuznets (1934) created national income accounting
— a measurement system that made macroeconomics
possible. Before the 1930s, no one even knew
how big their economy was.
🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Animal spirits / 动物精神: Keynes' term for the psychological forces driving investment — confidence, fear, herd behavior. "A large proportion of our positive activities depend on spontaneous optimism rather than mathematical expectation." When animal spirits collapse, so does investment — even if the fundamental economic situation hasn't changed.
  • The Depression mentality / 萧条心态: people who lived through the Depression developed lifelong frugality, distrust of banks, and preference for tangible assets. This generational psychological scar shaped economic behavior for decades — a reminder that economic psychology has long half-lives.
  • Hyperinflation trauma / 恶性通胀创伤: the German collective memory of 1923 explains Germany's uniquely hawkish monetary policy today. The ECB's singular focus on price stability is a direct descendant of Weimar trauma. Historical experience shapes economic psychology across generations.
  • Paradox of thrift / 节俭悖论: what is rational for an individual (saving more when times are uncertain) is disastrous for the collective (less spending → less income → less ability to save). The gap between micro rationality and macro consequences is a recurring theme — and a deeply counterintuitive one.
