Golden Age / 黄金时代

The belle époque of capitalism: the gold standard unified global finance, the "marginal revolution" transformed economic theory, and the first era of globalization connected the world more deeply than ever before — until war shattered everything.

📈 Economics / 经济学

The Marginal Revolution / 边际革命

In the 1870s, three economists independently discovered the same idea: value is determined at the margin, not by total labor. William Stanley Jevons (England), Carl Menger (Austria), and Léon Walras (France) each argued that the value of a good depends on its marginal utility — the utility of one additional unit — not the total utility of all units. This resolved the diamond-water paradox (why are diamonds, which are useless for survival, more expensive than water, which is essential? Because water is abundant → marginal utility is low; diamonds are scarce → marginal utility is high) and transformed economics from a philosophical discipline into a mathematical one.

Alfred Marshall: The Great Synthesis / 马歇尔的综合

Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) unified classical and marginalist thinking. He gave us the supply-demand diagram still used in every economics textbook, the concept of elasticity, consumer and producer surplus, and the distinction between short-run and long-run equilibrium. Marshall also insisted that economics is a "science of human behavior" and kept his mathematics in footnotes — a modesty later economists abandoned.

Veblen and Institutional Economics / 凡勃伦

Thorstein Veblen's The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899) introduced conspicuous consumption — the idea that people buy goods not just for their utility but to signal status. Veblen attacked the rational actor model and argued that institutions, not individual choices, drive economic outcomes. He was the original heterodox economist — brilliant, unemployable, and fundamentally right that culture and institutions matter more than orthodox theory admitted.

The marginal revolution marked the point where economics became a fully mathematical discipline. Walras's general equilibrium model — all markets clearing simultaneously — remains the theoretical benchmark, even though its assumptions are wildly unrealistic.
⚡ Politics / 政治

The Gold Standard as Anchor / 金本位作为锚定

By the 1870s, most major economies had adopted the classical gold standard: each currency was convertible to a fixed quantity of gold, which meant exchange rates between currencies were essentially fixed. This created remarkable stability — but at a cost. Under the gold standard, a country's money supply was determined by its gold reserves, not by domestic economic needs. Defending the gold parity required painful adjustments: when gold flowed out, money contracted, interest rates rose, and economic activity slowed. The gold standard was a self-regulating system — but the "self-regulation" often meant recessions.

Imperialism and the Global Economy / 帝国主义与全球经济

The period 1870–1914 was the peak of European imperialism. The "Scramble for Africa" (1881–1914) divided the continent among European powers. Empires provided raw materials (rubber, copper, cotton), captive markets for manufactured goods, and investment opportunities for European capital. This was globalization with gunboats — the economic integration of 1870–1914 was real, but it was built on coercion, not consent.

The Rise of Labor / 劳工的崛起

Trade unions gained legal recognition (UK 1871, Germany 1890s). Socialist parties entered parliaments. Bismarck pioneered the welfare state in Germany (1880s) — accident insurance, health insurance, old-age pensions — not from socialist conviction, but to preempt the socialists. The economics was sound: social insurance reduced labor conflict and increased productivity. The politics was cynical but effective.

📜 History / 历史
1870s
Marginal Revolution
Jevons, Menger, Walras independently develop marginal utility theory. Economics becomes mathematical.
1873–1896
Long Depression
Prolonged deflation (falling prices) in Europe and US. Real wages rose. Called a "depression" by contemporaries but increasingly questioned by modern historians.
1890
Sherman Antitrust Act (USA)
First major antitrust law. Response to the trusts (Standard Oil, railroads) that dominated the US economy.
1890
Marshall's Principles
The textbook that defined economics for a generation. Supply-demand diagrams, elasticity, surplus.
1907
Panic of 1907
Bank run triggered by failed copper speculation. J.P. Morgan personally organized a private bailout. Led to the creation of the Federal Reserve (1913).
1913
Federal Reserve founded
US finally gets a central bank — the last major economy to do so. Designed as a decentralized system to placate fears of Wall Street dominance.
1914
World War I begins
The golden age ends. Gold standard suspended. Governments take control of economies. The belle époque is over.
💰 Finance / 金融

First Globalization of Capital / 第一次资本全球化

By 1914, foreign investment had reached levels (~30% of GDP in capital-exporting countries) not seen again until the 1990s. Britain alone invested ~£4 billion abroad — Argentina's railways, American cattle ranches, Indian infrastructure. The income from these investments (~9% of British GDP by 1913) made Britain a "rentier" nation — living partly on returns from past investment, a preview of the modern financialized economy.

J.P. Morgan and the Banker Era / 摩根时代

Before the Fed, J.P. Morgan was America's de facto central bank. In the Panic of 1907, Morgan locked the presidents of the major trust companies in his library until they agreed to a coordinated bailout. This worked — but the spectacle of one private citizen holding the financial system together convinced Congress that America needed a real central bank.

The Gold Standard's Financial Logic

Under the gold standard, central banks were supposed to follow the "rules of the game": raise rates when gold flowed out, lower them when gold flowed in. In practice, they often sterilized flows (offsetting gold movements with domestic operations) — cheating on the rules to avoid domestic pain. The gold standard worked because the major powers cooperated to maintain it, not because it was automatic.

📐 Mathematics / 数学

Walrasian General Equilibrium / 瓦尔拉斯一般均衡

n markets, n prices: p₁, p₂, ..., pₙ

For each good i:
  D_i(p₁...pₙ) = S_i(p₁...pₙ)

Walras' Law: Σ p_i·D_i = Σ p_i·S_i
  → If n−1 markets clear, the nth must too

Existence of equilibrium proven by
Arrow & Debreu (1954) using fixed-point theorem
— but computational intractable for realistic n

Marshallian Elasticity / 马歇尔弹性

Price Elasticity of Demand:
  η = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q)

|η| > 1: elastic (luxury goods)
|η| < 1: inelastic (necessities)

Consumer Surplus = Area under demand curve above price
Producer Surplus = Area above supply curve below price

Deadweight loss from taxation:
  DWL = ½ × tax × ΔQ
🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Conspicuous consumption / 炫耀性消费: Veblen's insight that consumption signals status. The wealthy buy goods precisely because they are expensive — not despite it. This violates the law of demand (higher price should mean lower quantity demanded) and anticipates modern behavioral economics by a century.
  • Belle Époque optimism / 美好年代乐观主义: the psychological atmosphere of 1870–1914 was one of relentless progress — technology, wealth, knowledge all advancing. The gold standard, telegraph, steamship, and railway created a sense that the world was converging toward civilization and prosperity. This optimism made the catastrophe of WWI psychologically devastating — the "end of innocence" that shaped 20th-century pessimism.
  • The Panic of 1907 as mass psychology: no fundamental economic disaster triggered the 1907 panic — it was a crisis of confidence. Once depositors feared banks would fail, they withdrew funds, causing those banks to fail. The self-fulfilling prophecy of bank runs demonstrated that finance operates on psychology as much as arithmetic.
