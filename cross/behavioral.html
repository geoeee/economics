Behavioral Economics / 行为经济学

What happens when you replace the rational Homo economicus with actual humans — biased, emotional, and magnificently irrational?

📈 Economics / 经济学

Prospect Theory / 前景理论

Kahneman & Tversky's (1979) Nobel-winning challenge to expected utility theory. People don't evaluate outcomes in absolute terms — they evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point. And losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good (loss aversion).

Prospect Theory Value Function:
• Concave for gains (risk-averse when winning)
• Convex for losses (risk-seeking when losing — "double or nothing")
• Steeper for losses than gains (loss aversion, λ ≈ 2.25)
This explains why investors hold losing stocks too long and sell winners too early — the disposition effect.

System 1 vs System 2 / 系统一与系统二

Kahneman's dual-process model: System 1 is fast, automatic, emotional, effortless. System 2 is slow, deliberate, logical, effortful. Most economic decisions use System 1 — which is prone to systematic biases. This is why people make the same financial mistakes repeatedly.

⚡ Politics / 政治

Nudge Theory / 助推理论

Thaler & Sunstein's libertarian paternalism: design choice architecture to guide people toward better decisions without restricting freedom. Default enrollment in retirement savings (opt-out vs opt-in) increases participation from ~40% to ~90%. The UK's Behavioural Insights Team ("Nudge Unit") applied this to tax collection, organ donation, and public health.

Behavioral Public Policy

If people are predictably irrational, should policy protect them from themselves? Sugar taxes, mandatory cooling-off periods for loans, plain-packaging for cigarettes — all justified by behavioral insights. Critics warn of a slippery slope toward technocratic manipulation.

💰 Finance / 金融

Behavioral finance explains market anomalies that efficient-market theory can't: bubbles, crashes, momentum, and the value premium.

  • Disposition effect: selling winners too early, holding losers too long — driven by loss aversion and regret avoidance
  • Home bias: investors massively overweight domestic stocks despite diversification benefits — familiarity feels safer
  • Herding: following the crowd; fund managers buy what others buy to avoid career risk from being different
  • Excessive trading: overconfident investors trade more and earn lower returns (Barber & Odean, 2000)
  • Dividend preference: irrational preference for cash dividends over equivalent capital gains — mental accounting treats income and capital differently
📐 Mathematics / 数学

Prospect Theory Value Function / 前景理论价值函数

v(x) =  x^α        if x ≥ 0  (gains)
v(x) = −λ(−x)^β   if x < 0  (losses)

Typical estimates: α ≈ β ≈ 0.88, λ ≈ 2.25

Diminishing sensitivity in both directions.
Losses weigh ~2.25× more than equal-sized gains.

Probability Weighting / 概率加权

w(p) = p^γ / (p^γ + (1−p)^γ)^(1/γ)

γ ≈ 0.65: overweight small probabilities (lottery tickets, insurance)
underweight moderate/high probabilities

Hyperbolic Discounting / 双曲线贴现

V = A / (1 + kD)

A = reward amount, D = delay, k = discount factor
Today:  $100 now vs $120 in 1 month → take $100
Future: $100 in 12 months vs $120 in 13 months → take $120

Time-inconsistent preferences: we plan to be patient tomorrow,
but when tomorrow arrives, we're impulsive again.
🧠 Psychology / 心理
  • Mental accounting / 心理账户: we put money into separate mental "buckets" (salary, bonus, tax refund, inheritance) and treat them differently. This is economically irrational — money is fungible — but psychologically universal.
  • Endowment effect / 禀赋效应: we value things more once we own them. Mug experiments: people demand ~$7 to sell a mug they were given, but would only pay ~$3 to buy it. This is loss aversion applied to ownership.
  • Sunk cost fallacy / 沉没成本谬误: continuing to invest in failing projects because you've already invested so much. "I've already spent $200 fixing this car, I can't give up now" — leading to $300 more. Rationally, sunk costs should be ignored.
  • Social proof / 社会认同: we look to others to determine correct behavior. This drives everything from fashion trends to bank runs to stock market bubbles.
